Bachelor’s thesis

How do firms navigate market uncertainty under changing market conditions?

A case study of the U.S. electric vehicle market, written with Dorna Salmani and supervised by Mikael Hilmersson at the School of Business, Economics and Law, spring 2026. The market moved quickly from a predictable growth trajectory to tariff escalation, removed incentives and regulatory unpredictability. We interviewed people across the automotive value chain to see how firms actually responded.

9 semi-structured interviews U.S. EV market Automotive value chain Dynamic capabilities Sensemaking

Published in GUPEA, the University of Gothenburg’s open archive.

Read the thesis on GUPEA ↗

One main question, answered through three sub-questions. Our answers, in short:

Main question

How do firms navigate market uncertainty under changing market conditions?

As a continuous loop. They gather signals from many sources, filter them through attention and framing that depend on their position, make sense of them, and respond with a mix of moves, limited by what they have already committed to. Under deep uncertainty, navigating is less about bold bets and more about keeping options open. We call this defensive reconfiguring.

  1. Sub-question 1

    What types of market signals do firms identify and consider when interpreting changing market conditions?

    Many, from several sources at once: industry publications, internal data, dealer and customer feedback, financial indicators and competitors’ moves. Which signals matter most depends on where an actor sits. Strategy and product roles watch policy and regulation, retailers watch customer behaviour, and financiers watch how vehicles already on the road perform.

  2. Sub-question 2

    How are these signals perceived and interpreted under conditions of uncertainty?

    Through scanning, attention, framing and sensemaking, all shaped by position rather than chance. Scanning has intensified but stays selective. No one saw developments as purely a threat or purely an opportunity, and interpretation aims for a plausible reading rather than a final answer. When people read the same development differently, it’s because they sit in different places, not because some analyse better.

  3. Sub-question 3

    How do firms navigate and respond to uncertain and evolving market conditions in practice?

    Not with one strategy but with several responses at once: deferring, reconfiguring, avoiding, committing in stages, adapting and trying to influence conditions. What a firm can do is limited by what it has already built. A focused niche player can pivot more freely than a broad manufacturer weighed down by earlier investments and dealer networks.

International Business Live Case, spring 2026, with Andreas Lundahl, Ashley Jackson, Makda Tiruneh and Rania Benbouchta

Taking Teddykompaniet abroad

France Italy Spain Portugal

A live case for Teddykompaniet, a Swedish maker of premium soft toys. The brief: find the best European market for its first real step abroad, and decide how to enter it.

We compared France, Italy, Spain and Portugal on six dimensions (market demand, size, growth, purchasing power, market conditions and business environment), weighting them with the Analytic Hierarchy Process. France came out on top thanks to its market size, strong purchasing power and favourable conditions.

We recommended a phased, distributor-based entry with a premium-oriented partner, starting in curated French retailers such as Natalys and Smallable. Success depends on choosing the right distributor, staying in premium channels and protecting the brand rather than scaling fast.

EKR112 Commercial Law, with Nicholas Tilk

Blockchain in Trade Documentation

This paper looks at how blockchain could modernise global trade documents, especially electronic bills of lading, by tackling the inefficiency, fraud risk and legal uncertainty of paper-based processes.

We assessed legal frameworks such as UNCITRAL’s Model Law on Electronic Transferable Records and the UK Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023, and case law including Glencore v MSC, where traditional ideas of possession and control get complicated.

Platforms like Bolero and TradeLens show the problem of fragmented “digital islands”. Wider adoption needs interoperable standards, legal harmonisation and investment in infrastructure, particularly in developing nations.
